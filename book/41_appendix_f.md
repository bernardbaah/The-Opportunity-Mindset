# Appendix F: Glossary of Opportunity Terms

*The Opportunity Mindset · Bernard Baah · Filly Coder AI Future Series*

---

Plain-language definitions of 65 key terms used throughout this book, listed in alphabetical order. Where a term has a specific meaning in the context of this book's frameworks, that meaning is prioritized. General definitions follow where additional context is helpful.

---

**Abundance Mindset**
The belief that value, opportunity, and success are not finite resources that deplete when shared. Contrasted with the Scarcity Mindset. An abundance orientation supports collaboration, generosity, and long-term thinking by reducing the perceived cost of helping others.

**Adjacent Possible**
A concept borrowed from complexity theorist Stuart Kauffman and applied by Steven Johnson: the set of next possibilities that become accessible once a current state is reached. In opportunity terms, every move you make opens a new set of adjacent possible moves. Opportunity compound because each built thing makes new things possible.

**Agency**
The capacity to act independently and make your own choices. In the context of the Opportunity Mindset, agency is the active exercise of personal responsibility for one's outcomes — not the denial of external constraints, but the refusal to be entirely defined by them.

**Asymmetric Opportunity**
An opportunity where the potential upside is significantly larger than the potential downside. The Opportunity Equation seeks to identify these: situations where risk is bounded and potential return is not. Most transformative opportunities are asymmetric when approached correctly.

**Attention Economy**
The competitive landscape in which human attention is the scarce resource being competed for by media, platforms, and content creators. Understanding the attention economy is relevant for opportunity creation in any domain that requires reaching an audience.

**Beginner's Mind (Shoshin)**
A concept from Zen Buddhism referring to approaching familiar subjects with openness, lack of preconception, and eagerness — as a beginner would. In expert practitioners, maintaining beginner's mind prevents the accumulation of expertise from becoming a limitation on creative possibility.

**Black Swan**
A term popularized by Nassim Nicholas Taleb describing a rare, high-impact event that is outside the realm of regular expectation and yet is rationalized with hindsight as having been predictable. Relevant to opportunity evaluation: building robust, flexible opportunity systems is more valuable than trying to predict specific rare events.

**Capability Gap**
The distance between the skills and knowledge you currently have and those required to execute a specific opportunity. Identifying and closing capability gaps is one of the primary uses of the Learn stage in the Opportunity Flywheel.

**Cognitive Bias**
A systematic error in thinking that affects decisions and judgments. Key cognitive biases relevant to opportunity work include: confirmation bias (favoring information that confirms existing beliefs), availability bias (overweighting recent or memorable events), and survivorship bias (drawing conclusions only from visible successes while ignoring invisible failures).

**Compounding**
The mechanism by which growth builds on itself over time. Most relevant in opportunity terms when applied to knowledge (each thing learned makes new things easier to learn), relationships (trust and reputation accumulate over time), and financial assets. The Opportunity Flywheel is designed to enable compounding across all these dimensions simultaneously.

**Confirmation Bias**
The tendency to search for and favor information that confirms one's existing beliefs. Particularly dangerous in opportunity evaluation, where it can lead practitioners to accept evidence supporting an opportunity thesis while dismissing contradictory evidence.

**Creative Destruction**
A term from economist Joseph Schumpeter describing the process by which innovation displaces existing industries, companies, and jobs while creating new ones. Understanding creative destruction helps opportunity practitioners position themselves on the right side of structural economic transitions.

**Deliberate Practice**
A concept from psychologist Anders Ericsson describing a specific type of practice: focused, feedback-rich repetition in areas just beyond current competence. Distinguished from mere repetition or experience accumulation. The most reliable method for developing real expertise across any domain.

**Digital Leverage**
The amplification of individual effort through digital tools — the ability to reach, serve, or influence far more people at far lower marginal cost than was possible with pre-digital methods. AI tools represent the most significant expansion of digital leverage in the current era.

**Ecosystem**
A network of interconnected actors — people, organizations, tools, platforms — that create mutual value through interaction. Opportunity Creators build ecosystems, not just products or careers. An ecosystem generates value continuously and at scale.

**Emergent Property**
A characteristic of a system that arises from the interaction of its components rather than from any individual component. Relevant in community and network building: the value of a network is an emergent property of its members' interactions, not a simple sum of individual contributions.

**Entrepreneurial Mindset**
The combination of attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors associated with entrepreneurship: opportunity orientation, tolerance for ambiguity, action bias, comfort with risk, and a focus on value creation. Distinct from but overlapping with the Opportunity Mindset — entrepreneurship is one application of it.

**Execution Risk**
The risk that a plan will fail not because the opportunity is wrong but because the execution is flawed — poor decision-making, insufficient resources, team failure, or inadequate systems. Distinguished from market risk (the opportunity is not real) and timing risk (the opportunity is real but not yet available).

**First-Mover Advantage**
The competitive benefit of being the first entrant in a new market or category. Real but often overstated: fast followers who learn from first movers' mistakes frequently outperform pioneers. Understanding when first-mover advantage matters requires domain-specific judgment.

**Fixed Mindset**
Psychologist Carol Dweck's term for the belief that capabilities are innate, fixed, and not meaningfully improvable through effort. The opposite of the Growth Mindset. A Fixed Mindset is incompatible with the development arc described in the Opportunity Skills Framework.

**Flywheel**
A mechanical metaphor for a self-reinforcing system that, once in motion, sustains and accelerates its own movement. Jim Collins applied it to business strategy. In this book, the Opportunity Flywheel describes the six-stage cycle — Discover, Learn, Build, Connect, Create, Reinvest — through which practitioners build compounding opportunity-generation systems.

**Friction**
Any force that slows or stops movement from intention to action. Can be external (structural barriers, resource constraints, market conditions) or internal (fear, habit, unclear thinking). Managing friction is a core execution skill in the Opportunity Mindset framework.

**Gig Economy**
A labor market characterized by short-term contracts and freelance work rather than permanent employment. Relevant as both an opportunity context (freelance platforms enabling new income streams) and a structural shift (reducing the stability of traditional employment while expanding individual flexibility).

**Growth Mindset**
Psychologist Carol Dweck's term for the belief that capabilities can be developed through dedication and effort. The foundational mental model for the Opportunity Mindset, and the prerequisite for the development arc described across the book's frameworks.

**Human Capital**
The economic value of a person's skills, knowledge, experience, and capabilities. Investing in human capital — through education, skill development, deliberate practice, and diverse experience — is one of the highest-return activities available to individuals over a career.

**Hypothesis Testing**
The practice of forming specific, testable predictions about whether an opportunity is real, and designing low-cost experiments to confirm or refute them before committing significant resources. Borrowed from scientific method; essential for rigorous opportunity evaluation.

**Imposter Syndrome**
The persistent internal experience of feeling fraudulent despite evidence of competence and achievement. Particularly common among high-achievers from underrepresented backgrounds. Not a personality defect but a common psychological response to operating in environments where you lack visible models who look like you.

**Information Asymmetry**
A situation where one party in a transaction or competition has more or better information than the other. Identifying and exploiting information asymmetry — knowing something your competitors don't — is one source of opportunity advantage.

**Intrapreneur**
An individual who acts with entrepreneurial initiative within an organization rather than founding their own. Intrapreneurs identify internal opportunities, propose and build new initiatives, and drive organizational change while leveraging existing organizational resources.

**Jobs-to-Be-Done (JTBD)**
A framework developed by Clayton Christensen describing why people "hire" products and services: not for features but to accomplish specific functional, emotional, or social jobs. Understanding the jobs a customer is trying to get done is one of the most reliable methods for identifying genuine, persistent unmet needs.

**Knowledge Economy**
An economy in which the primary driver of value creation is knowledge, information, and intellectual capability rather than physical goods or manual labor. Practitioners with rare, high-quality knowledge enjoy growing leverage in the knowledge economy.

**Lateral Thinking**
Edward de Bono's term for a problem-solving approach that uses indirect, creative reasoning to reach solutions that sequential, analytical thinking would not produce. Relevant to opportunity creation: connecting disparate domains often requires deliberate lateral thinking rather than domain expertise alone.

**Legacy**
The lasting impact of a person's life and work on others — what persists after their active involvement ends. In the book's framework, legacy is not a retrospective concern but a present-tense orientation: designing work, relationships, and institutions to create value that compounds over time.

**Leverage**
The ability to produce disproportionate results relative to inputs. Leverage can come from technology, capital, networks, media, code, and knowledge. Naval Ravikant's formulation: wealth is built through leverage, and each type of leverage has different characteristics. Identifying and applying the right leverage for a given opportunity is a core Opportunity Creator skill.

**Luck Surface Area**
The observation (attributed to Jason Roberts) that the chance of a fortunate event occurring is proportional to both what you do (actions you take) and how many people know about it (visibility). "Luck = Action × Reach." The implication: practitioners can engineer more good fortune by taking more action and building broader visibility.

**Market Risk**
The risk that an opportunity is fundamentally based on an incorrect assumption about market demand — that the need is not real, large enough, or persistent enough to support the proposed solution. The most fundamental risk in new venture creation; often the one most overlooked by founders in love with their own idea.

**Minimum Viable Product (MVP)**
The version of a new product or service that includes only the features necessary to test the core hypothesis and gather learning from real users. An MVP is not a rough draft — it is a deliberate tool for generating validated learning with minimal waste.

**Network Effect**
A phenomenon where a product or service becomes more valuable as more people use it. Classic examples include telephone networks, social platforms, and marketplaces. Understanding network effects is important for opportunity creators building platforms, communities, or ecosystems.

**Opportunity Audit**
A structured inventory of one's current situation — assets, gaps, friction, and momentum — as a prerequisite to opportunity identification and pursuit. Described in detail in Appendix B. The Opportunity Audit grounds planning in accurate self-knowledge rather than aspiration.

**Opportunity Canvas**
A one-page visual planning tool for mapping a specific opportunity across its key dimensions: unmet need, target beneficiary, unique position, key resources, key actions, risks, and success metrics. Used to quickly capture and communicate an opportunity thesis. Described in Appendix (Bonus Resources).

**Opportunity Compass**
A directional framework for identifying which type of opportunity — Growth (North), Income (South), Reach (East), or Impact (West) — is most aligned with a practitioner's current situation, goals, and resources. Described in Appendix B. Helps practitioners prioritize the right type of opportunity for where they are in their development arc.

**Opportunity Creator**
The most advanced of the three Opportunity Readiness profiles (see Appendix D). An Opportunity Creator generates opportunities not just for themselves but for others, at scale, through systems and ecosystems that continue producing value independently of ongoing personal effort.

**Opportunity Equation**
The book's core evaluation formula: Opportunity Value = (Unmet Need × Your Unique Position) ÷ (Risk × Time to Value). A thinking tool, not a precise mathematical calculation. Helps practitioners compare opportunities and identify where to focus development effort to improve their competitive position.

**Opportunity Flywheel**
The book's central integrating framework describing how practitioners build self-reinforcing opportunity-generation systems. The six stages: Discover (surface signals), Learn (develop capability), Build (create artifacts), Connect (build relationships), Create (generate new opportunities), Reinvest (return resources to the system).

**Opportunity Log**
A regular written record of signals, observations, problems, patterns, and ideas noticed in daily life. A foundational habit for developing opportunity recognition skills. Reviewed weekly to identify recurring themes and potential opportunity areas.

**Opportunity Mindset**
The central concept of this book: a set of beliefs, skills, habits, and practices that enable an individual to consistently identify, create, and act on opportunities — not as isolated events but as a compounding system. Distinguished from mere optimism by its emphasis on structured capability development and systematic practice.

**Opportunity Multiplication**
The fifth skill domain in the Opportunity Skills Framework: the ability to leverage initial opportunities to generate multiple subsequent ones, turning singular wins into compounding systems. The Reinvest stage of the Opportunity Flywheel operationalizes this concept.

**Opportunity Seeker**
The entry-level of the three Opportunity Readiness profiles (see Appendix D). An Opportunity Seeker is motivated and searching but has not yet built the systems that convert opportunity recognition into consistent capture and execution.

**Opportunity Sprint**
A 30-day focused commitment to a single opportunity: one specific, measurable initiative with defined milestones. Used as the primary output of the Practitioner Guide in Appendix B.

**Optionality**
The value of having future choices available without being committed to any particular path. Certain investments — skill development, relationship building, financial security — increase optionality by expanding the range of opportunities available later. Some practitioners over-optimize for optionality and never commit; commitment itself is what generates the feedback needed to progress.

**Pre-Mortem**
A decision analysis technique where a team or individual imagines that a project has already failed and works backward to identify what caused the failure. A reliable method for surfacing risks that forward-looking analysis misses. Pioneered by psychologist Gary Klein.

**Psychological Safety**
The belief that one can speak up, take risks, make mistakes, and share ideas without punishment or humiliation. Originally studied in team contexts by Amy Edmondson. Relevant to the internal conditions required for taking the kinds of risks that opportunity capture requires.

**Resourcefulness**
The ability to find creative ways to solve problems and move forward with limited resources. Distinguished from resource abundance: resourcefulness is a skill that can be developed regardless of resource constraints, and it is often more predictive of entrepreneurial success than access to capital.

**Risk Tolerance**
An individual's capacity to accept potential loss or negative outcomes in pursuit of potential gains. Risk tolerance is not fixed — it varies with domain, stakes, preparation level, and prior experience. Building genuine capability in a domain typically raises effective risk tolerance within that domain.

**Scarcity Mindset**
The belief that opportunity, value, and success are finite — that someone else's gain is necessarily one's own loss. A zero-sum orientation incompatible with the Opportunity Mindset. Scarcity thinking inhibits collaboration, reduces generosity, and distorts opportunity evaluation toward excessive risk aversion.

**Signal-to-Noise Ratio**
The proportion of useful, actionable information (signal) to irrelevant or misleading information (noise) in any information environment. Developing a high personal signal-to-noise ratio — through selective information diets, curated sources, and active filtering — is a prerequisite for reliable opportunity recognition.

**Social Capital**
The value that comes from social relationships, networks, and community membership. Includes the norms of reciprocity, trust, and cooperation that make networks function productively. Social capital is built over time through consistent generosity, reliability, and genuine contribution.

**Structural Opportunity**
An opportunity created by large-scale structural changes — demographic shifts, technological transitions, regulatory changes, or macroeconomic trends — rather than small gaps in existing markets. Structural opportunities typically have longer windows and greater scale potential than tactical opportunities.

**Systems Thinking**
A way of analyzing problems and opportunities that focuses on the relationships, feedback loops, and emergent behaviors of complex systems rather than isolated components. Relevant to the Opportunity Flywheel: treating opportunity as a system, not a series of unrelated events, is what separates the Opportunity Creator from the Opportunity Seeker.

**Timing Risk**
The risk that an opportunity is fundamentally real but that the market or technology is not yet ready for the solution. Many failed companies were too early, not wrong. Evaluating timing risk — often the hardest risk to assess — requires judgment about the pace of external change relative to the window available.

**Unique Insight**
The specific, non-obvious observation or piece of knowledge that an opportunity thesis is built on. The best opportunities are often invisible to most observers precisely because they require a unique insight — combining domain knowledge, lived experience, or a specific information asymmetry — to see them.

**Value Creation**
The process of generating something useful that did not exist before. In economics, value creation is what increases overall wealth; it is not merely redistributing existing value. The Opportunity Mindset is fundamentally oriented toward value creation rather than value extraction.

**Value Proposition**
The specific benefit that a product, service, or initiative provides to a defined group of people, and why it is superior to alternatives. A clear value proposition is the output of rigorous opportunity evaluation: it answers "who benefits, how, and why us?"

**Venture Capital (VC)**
A form of private equity financing provided to early-stage, high-growth-potential companies in exchange for equity. Relevant as both a source of capital for qualifying startups and as a signal — companies attracting VC investment often indicate where structural opportunities are being recognized by sophisticated market observers.

**Weak Ties**
Sociologist Mark Granovetter's concept: the relationships in one's network that are not close or frequent (acquaintances, former colleagues, distant connections) are often the source of the most novel information and unexpected opportunities. Strong ties tend to share the same information; weak ties bridge different information ecosystems.

**Zero-Sum Thinking**
The belief that gains for one party necessarily come at the expense of another — that value cannot be created, only redistributed. Empirically inaccurate in most economic and social contexts; psychologically common. Overcoming zero-sum thinking is a prerequisite for collaboration-based opportunity creation.

---

*Definitions in this glossary reflect usage within the book's frameworks. Where terms have multiple technical definitions across disciplines, the most operationally relevant meaning for the Opportunity Mindset practitioner has been prioritized.*
